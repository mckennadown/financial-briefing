Developments
Canada

The TSX jumps nearly 900 points and ends a seven-day losing streak

What happened: The TSX closed at 34,082.12 on Monday. That's up 892.27 points, or 2.7%. MT Newswires said the gain ended a seven-day losing streak that had pushed the index to a six-month low. Gold rose US$136.00 to US$3,651.40 an ounce, The Canadian Press reported. Gold, mining and tech stocks led the way up.
Why it matters: "The TSX is getting a double boost from both stronger gold prices and renewed enthusiasm for technology," analyst Anish Chopra told CP. But he warned that the worries behind last week's drop haven't gone away: "I don't think today takes away from that."
What it means for you: Monday won back more than a quarter of what the TSX lost since Nov. 11. Big up days often come right after big down days. That's why investors who sell in a panic often miss the rebound. Expect more big swings for a while.
US / Global

Nvidia brings back Jensen Huang as CEO, and US stocks soar

What happened: Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive, effective immediately, 24/7 Wall St. reported. Huang's exit as CEO the week before had set off a big sell-off in tech stocks. On Monday, Nvidia's shares jumped about 31%. The S&P 500 rose 4.7% and the Nasdaq rose 6.5%. CNBC said it was the Nasdaq's best day since April 2025.
Why it matters: Nvidia is so big that it moves the whole market. CP noted that a 1% move in Nvidia's stock moved the S&P 500 more than a 1% move in all but two of the other companies in the index. Monday's gain won back just over half of the S&P 500's 8.6% drop last week. Early Tuesday, CNBC reported that the Nasdaq was falling again as Nvidia gave back part of its gain.
What it means for you: If you own a US index fund, a lot of your money depends on a few giant tech companies. That's fine for long-term investors. Just know that news about one company can now swing your whole portfolio.
Canada

Macklem speaks today with inflation stuck at 3.1%

What happened: Bank of Canada Governor Tiff Macklem speaks to the Economic Club of Canada in Toronto today. The Bank's title for the speech is "Canada's monetary policy framework: A trusted anchor in uncertain waters." It's his first major speech since saying he will step down in June 2027. Last week, Statistics Canada said prices were up 3.1% from a year earlier in October, the same as in September, CP reported. Higher gas and grocery prices pushed inflation up.
Why it matters: The Bank aims to keep inflation at 2%. It raised its key rate to 2.50% on Oct. 28 to fight rising prices. But the economy is slowing. An early estimate put third-quarter growth at 1.3% (annualized), CP reported, below the Bank's 2.0% forecast. CP said that raised worries about another rate hike in December. The Bank has to balance high prices against a weak economy.
What it means for you: If you have a variable-rate mortgage or a line of credit, today's speech may hint at whether rates will rise again on Dec. 9. Savers, on the other hand, are getting slightly better rates on savings accounts and GICs.
VC / Private Markets

Canada puts $100 million into NATO's venture fund for defence startups

What happened: Canada has joined the NATO Innovation Fund, BetaKit reported. It's the 25th allied country to do so. It's investing €64.6 million, or about $100 million. The Business Development Bank of Canada (BDC), a federal Crown corporation, will manage the stake. The fund has raised more than €1.1 billion. It puts €3 million to €15 million into each young tech company with defence and security uses.
Why it matters: Canada promised to join back in 2024 but only did so last weekend. Joining gives Canadian defence startups another way to raise money. It also fits Ottawa's plan to spend more on defence. BDC grew its own defence investment program to $6 billion in March.
What it means for you: Defence tech is one of the fastest-growing corners of Canadian venture capital (money invested in young private companies). More money flowing in could mean more skilled tech jobs in Canada.

Learn One Thing Today
Annualized Rate
An "annualized" rate shows what a short-term change would add up to if it kept going at the same pace for a full year. Canada's economy is measured every three months. So when the early estimate says the economy grew 1.3% "annualized" in the third quarter, it actually grew only about 0.3% in those three months. That pace, kept up for four quarters, works out to about 1.3% a year. Annualizing makes it easy to compare numbers measured over different lengths of time.
