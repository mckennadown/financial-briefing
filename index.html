Developments
Canada

Canada's inflation held at 3.1% in October, a little below forecasts

What happened: Statistics Canada said prices rose 3.1% over the past year. That's the same as September. Economists had expected 3.2%. Without energy, prices rose 2.0%, down from 2.1%. The Bank of Canada's preferred "core" measures stayed close to its 2% target. One was 1.8% and another was 2.0%.
Why it matters: High gas prices are doing most of the damage. Those prices are driven by the war in the Middle East. Prices for most other things are rising at a normal pace. CIBC noted inflation is well below the Bank of Canada's own forecast of 3.4% for the last three months of the year.
What it means for you: Gas and energy bills are still the main strain on budgets. Most other prices aren't speeding up. That's a sign the cost-of-living squeeze isn't spreading, at least for now.
Canada

Will the Bank of Canada raise rates again? Experts are split

What happened: On Nov 10, the Bank released notes from its October meeting, when it raised its key rate to 2.5%. The notes said more hikes "might be needed." The Bank expects inflation to reach 3.5% by the end of 2026. After today's report, traders still saw roughly even odds of a hike on Dec 9.
Why it matters: Economists disagree. Capital Economics, CIBC, Citi and BMO expect the Bank to hold rates steady. Scotiabank and KPMG still expect a 0.25-point hike. BMO's Benjamin Reitzes said "there's nothing screaming rate hike in this report."
What it means for you: If you have a variable-rate mortgage or a line of credit, your payments depend on this decision. A hike would raise them. A pause would give some breathing room. Either way, plan your budget for rates staying near today's level or a bit higher.
US / Global

Tech stocks fell on Friday, and the loonie dipped below 70 US cents

What happened: By late morning Friday, the Nasdaq was down 229.82 points, led by tech stocks. The S&P 500 was down 30.73 points. The TSX was nearly flat, down 31.29 points. Oil was steady at about US$88.56 a barrel, and gold at US$3,886.30 an ounce. On Monday, the loonie slipped to 69.89 US cents after the inflation report.
Why it matters: US investors are worried that their central bank will raise rates in December. Higher US rates tend to make the US dollar more attractive than ours. The TSX held up better than US markets because it has fewer tech companies.
What it means for you: A loonie under 70 US cents makes cross-border shopping, US travel and imported goods pricier. If you're planning a US trip, it may be worth budgeting a bit extra.
VC / Private Markets

Federal budget offers a $15-million tax break for startup founders and investors

What happened: Budget 2026, tabled Nov 5, creates the "Canada Growth Incentive." It lets people avoid capital gains tax on up to $15 million in profit when they sell qualifying shares of a Canadian company. Ottawa estimates it will cost $715 million over four years. Conservative Leader Pierre Poilievre called it too complex. He said, "I can't figure out who actually qualifies."
Why it matters: The government says a founder with a $15-million gain would keep about 96% of the company's value when selling, up from about 71% today. The aim is to keep founders and their companies in Canada. It comes as startup funding is at its lowest level since 2021. Many sectors are excluded, including real estate, finance and farming. Detailed rules aren't out yet.
What it means for you: If you own shares in a private Canadian company, such as a startup that pays you partly in stock, this could cut your tax bill a lot if the company is sold. Wait for the final rules before planning around it.

Learn One Thing Today
Core Inflation
A measure of rising prices that strips out items whose prices jump around a lot, like gas and some foods. It shows the underlying trend instead of one-off spikes. The Bank of Canada uses measures like this, called "CPI-trim" and "CPI-median," to decide whether price increases are spreading through the economy. Here's why you should care: today, headline inflation is 3.1% but core measures are near 2%. That gap is the main reason experts are split on whether your borrowing costs will go up in December.
