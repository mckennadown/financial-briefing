Developments
Canada

TSX drops 2.1% as gold, metals and oil slide together

What happened: The TSX fell 737.25 points on Wednesday to 34,578.05. That's a more than four-month low and its fifth losing day in a row. Gold fell US$130.70 to US$3,744.20 an ounce. Oil fell US$3.08 to US$82.93 a barrel. Mining stocks took the biggest hits: Ero Copper fell 11.5% and Lundin Mining nearly 10%. A few areas rose, including Canadian Tire, up 2.4%.
Why it matters: The TSX is heavy in mining and energy companies. When gold, copper and oil all fall on the same day, Canada's market feels it more than the US one. The S&P 500 fell only 0.2%, and the Nasdaq actually rose 0.4%.
What it means for you: If you own a Canadian index fund, your balance likely dipped this week. Five straight down days feel bad but are not unusual. If you're investing for years, this is a reminder of why spreading money across sectors and countries helps.
US / Global

US Fed notes: almost all officials expect more rate hikes

What happened: The US Federal Reserve released notes (called "minutes") from its Oct 27–28 meeting on Wednesday. The Fed held its key rate at 3.75% to 4%. But two of twelve voters wanted a hike right away. The notes say "almost all participants" think further hikes will likely be needed to bring inflation back to 2%. A couple said a hike bigger than a quarter point could be considered in December if inflation data disappoint.
Why it matters: Higher US rates tend to pull money toward the US dollar. That can push the loonie down, which is already near 70 US cents. It can also nudge Canadian bond yields higher. The Fed's next decision is Dec 9, the same day as the Bank of Canada's.
What it means for you: A weaker loonie makes US trips, online shopping and imported goods pricier. If you're planning a winter trip south, it may be worth buying US dollars gradually rather than all at once.
Canada

Canada–US trade talks pick up again, but no deal yet

What happened: Three senior officials told CBC News that trade talks with the US are ramping up. Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc holds regular calls with US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. There is no formal deal on the table. US tariffs on cars and steel remain the main sticking points. Canada's chief negotiator, Janice Charette, said the US has shown "an unwillingness" to agree to fully tariff-free trade.
Why it matters: Earlier talks collapsed without a deal, and the US added more tariffs. Uncertainty over trade weighs on business investment and hiring. Baystreet.ca noted traders were weighing the trade talks during Wednesday's TSX sell-off.
What it means for you: If you work in autos, steel or a related industry, a deal would mean more job security. For everyone else, a deal could help the loonie and lower some prices. But officials are aiming for lower tariffs on fewer goods, not zero tariffs.
VC / Private Markets

Promise Robotics raises $40 million to build homes with robots

What happened: Toronto-based Promise Robotics raised $40 million in a Series B round, BetaKit reported. Montreal's White Star Capital led it. Radical Ventures, Horizons Ventures and PSP Investments (which manages federal public-sector pensions) also joined. The company uses AI software and robots to build wall and floor sections in its Calgary and Nisku, Alberta factories. Crews then assemble them on site.
Why it matters: Canada has pledged to build 500,000 homes a year, but the Canadian Housing Research Network estimates it's closer to 200,000. Factory-built homes are one way to close that gap. "Homebuilding can't scale on trade labour alone," said CEO Ramtin Attar. It's also a sizable later-stage round at a time when that kind of money has been scarce in Canada.
What it means for you: Faster, cheaper building could help with housing supply over time. And if you have a public-sector pension, part of your retirement money is now backing this kind of company.

Learn One Thing Today
Basis Point
A tiny unit used to describe changes in interest rates. One basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, or 0.01%. So 25 basis points is 0.25%, and 100 basis points is a full 1%. Professionals use it because "the rate went up 0.25 percentage points" is clunky and "went up 0.25%" can be confusing. Here's why you should care: the Fed's notes say a couple of officials would consider a hike of "more than 25 basis points" in December. On a $400,000 variable-rate mortgage, every 25 basis points adds roughly $1,000 a year in interest.
