Developments
Canada

Macklem says oil will keep pushing prices up, and bets on a December hike jump

What happened: Bank of Canada Governor Tiff Macklem spoke in Toronto on Tuesday. He said falling oil prices may leave inflation a bit below the Bank's October forecast of 3.5% by year-end. "The inflation forecast is a bit lower," he said. But he warned that businesses will likely pass more of their higher fuel and shipping costs on to shoppers over the next year. "This second-round effect of the oil shock will add to inflation persistence," he said.
Why it matters: Traders now see a 79% chance of a quarter-point rate hike on Dec. 9, up from about 63% on Monday, BNN Bloomberg reported, citing LSEG data. CIBC economist Avery Shenfeld said Macklem was "going out of his way to not show any changes in his thinking from October," when the Bank raised rates to 2.50%. Macklem also admitted the Bank was "too slow to recognize" how long inflation would stick around in the past.
What it means for you: If you have a variable-rate mortgage or a line of credit, plan for the chance that your payments rise in December. If you're renewing a fixed mortgage soon, talk to your lender now. Savers may see slightly better GIC rates.
Canada

The TSX slips as mining stocks fall, while the loonie gains

What happened: The TSX closed at 33,962.25 on Tuesday, down 119.87 points. The Canadian Press said losses in basic materials (mining and metals companies) led the drop. Gold fell US$89.50 to US$3,561.90 an ounce. In New York, the Nasdaq fell 356.02 points and the S&P 500 dipped 36.08 points. The Dow rose 423.39 points to 50,018.70.
Why it matters: Monday's big rebound didn't carry through. Gold gave back more than half of Monday's jump, and gold miners are a big part of the TSX. Meanwhile, the loonie rose to 69.95 US cents. A stronger chance of higher Canadian rates tends to make the dollar more attractive to investors.
What it means for you: A small down day after a huge up day is normal. If you're planning a US trip or buying in US dollars, the loonie's rise helps a little. Don't read too much into one day of moves.
US / Global

US trade chief says a deal must "accommodate" Canada, but talks are stuck on steel and cars

What happened: US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer told CNBC on Wednesday that talks are stuck on steel, aluminum and autos. "We want to make sure that North American trade in these products is beneficial to the United States," he said. But he added: "Obviously, there would have to be something that accommodates Canada as well." On Sunday, he told Fox News the two sides were "fairly close" to a deal, BNN Bloomberg reported.
Why it matters: Steel, aluminum and autos are some of Canada's biggest exports to the US, and tariffs have hit those industries hard. Greer also criticized Canada's recent deal with China, which lets in some Chinese electric vehicles, steel and aluminum. He said "that gives me pause." Canada trade adviser Flavio Volpe said Greer's word "accommodate" shows "the objective isn't zero sum," but that "every inch is contested."
What it means for you: A deal could protect jobs in Ontario's auto plants and in steel and aluminum towns. It could also calm the TSX and the loonie. Until then, expect trade headlines to keep moving markets.
VC / Private Markets

Vancouver wildfire-tech firm raises US$43 million, and Real Ventures is back with a new fund

What happened: SenseNet, a Vancouver startup that uses sensors, cameras and AI to spot wildfires early, raised US$43 million (C$62 million), BetaKit reported. That's US$32 million from investors plus US$11 million in loans from National Bank. Separately, Montréal's Real Ventures made a first close of $45 million on a new early-stage fund, aiming for $60 million. It's the firm's first new fund since 2017. Québec's government is putting in up to $30 million.
Why it matters: SenseNet plans to more than double its team, from 50 to 110 people, and expand in the US. Real Ventures is also restarting FounderFuel, a Montréal program for very young startups. It plans to invest $500,000 in each company. That's more money for founders at the riskiest, earliest stage, where funding has been hard to find.
What it means for you: Climate and safety tech is a growing field for Canadian jobs. Most people can't invest directly in these private funds. But your pension plan or a Crown agency like BDC (which backs Real) may already have money in them.

Learn One Thing Today
Second-Round Effects
"Second-round effects" are what happens after a price shock spreads. Say oil prices jump. The first-round effect is simple: gas costs more. The second round comes later. Trucking companies, farmers and airlines pay more for fuel, so they raise their own prices. Workers then ask for raises to keep up. That's how one shock can keep inflation high long after the original jump. Central banks don't usually react to the first round. They worry about the second round, because it can make high inflation stick. That's what Macklem warned about on Tuesday.
