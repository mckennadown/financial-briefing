Developments
Canada

Canada's unemployment rate hit a six-month high of 6.7%

What happened: Statistics Canada said the country lost about 4,700 jobs in October. Economists had expected a gain of about 13,600. The unemployment rate rose to 6.7%, up from 6.5%. This follows job losses of 68,000 in September and 42,000 in August.
Why it matters: Three months in a row of job losses means the economy is cooling. Private-sector jobs fell by 53,000. Alberta was hit hardest, with its unemployment rate jumping to 7.3%. Wage growth for permanent workers slowed to 2% from a year earlier.
What it means for you: Finding a new job may take longer, and raises may be smaller. It's a good time to keep an emergency fund topped up.
Canada

Bond markets are betting on another Bank of Canada rate hike

What happened: The interest rate on 10-year Government of Canada bonds rose to about 3.86% on Nov 13. That's up from a two-month low of 3.65% on Nov 9. Traders increasingly expect the Bank of Canada to raise rates again, because inflation hit 3.1% in September. That's above the Bank's 2% target.
Why it matters: The Bank is caught in a squeeze. Prices are rising too fast, but jobs are disappearing. Experts disagree on what comes next. For example, CIBC expects a pause on Dec 9 and a hike in January.
What it means for you: Fixed mortgage rates tend to follow bond yields. If you're shopping for a mortgage or renewing one soon, rates could creep up. Savers may see slightly better GIC rates.
US / Global

US prices rose faster than expected, and a US rate hike now looks likely

What happened: US consumer prices rose 0.4% in October. Experts had expected 0.3%. After the report, markets put an 85% chance on a 0.25-point US Federal Reserve rate hike at its Dec 8–9 meeting. They also put a 15% chance on a bigger 0.50-point hike.
Why it matters: Higher US interest rates pull money toward the US dollar. That tends to weaken the loonie. They also push up Canadian bond yields, which often move with US ones.
What it means for you: A weaker Canadian dollar makes US trips, online shopping in US dollars, and imported goods more expensive.
Global / Markets

Stocks slipped, gold tumbled, and oil climbed

What happened: On Nov 12, the TSX fell 274.53 points, led by mining stocks. US markets also closed lower. Gold dropped US$115.20 to US$3,885.30 an ounce. Oil rose US$2.21 to US$88.74 a barrel. The loonie fell to 70.15 US cents.
Why it matters: Canada's stock market has a lot of mining and energy companies. Big swings in gold and oil move the TSX more than most other markets.
What it means for you: Pricier oil usually means higher gas prices at the pump. If you invest, expect bumpy days. Don't overreact to any single one.
VC / Private Markets

Canadian startup funding is at its lowest level since 2021

What happened: Canadian startups raised $4.3 billion across 397 deals in the first nine months of 2026, according to the CVCA, an industry group. That's the lowest total for that period since 2021. Third-quarter funding of $1.35 billion was down 34% from a year earlier. Big buyouts, however, more than doubled to $13.7 billion.
Why it matters: Money is going to fewer, bigger deals. Six deals made up 59% of the third quarter's total. Young companies trying to grow are struggling most to raise cash. Ottawa recently announced $675 million to help fund those later-stage deals.
What it means for you: If you work at or with a startup, expect a tighter environment. There may be fewer hires and more cost-cutting until funding picks up.
What to Watch Next
Canada's October inflation numbers (later this month): another reading above 3% would strengthen the case for a Bank of Canada hike.
Dec 8–9 — US Federal Reserve meeting: markets expect a hike. A bigger-than-expected move could push the loonie lower and Canadian yields higher.
Dec 9 — Bank of Canada's next rate decision: will weak jobs numbers or high inflation win out? This sets the tone for borrowing costs into 2027.
Learn One Thing Today
Bond Yield
The yearly return you'd earn by buying a bond (a loan to a government or company) at today's price. When investors expect interest rates to rise, they demand more return, so yields go up. Here's why you should care: banks set many fixed mortgage rates based on Government of Canada bond yields, so rising yields often mean pricier mortgages a few weeks later.
