Developments
US / Global

Stocks slide again as worries about AI companies spread

What happened: The TSX fell more than 1% on Monday, to 35,722.13. Mining stocks led the drop. It kept falling Tuesday morning and was down 276.67 points by 11 a.m. ET. Tech stocks fell 2.33%, and software companies like Constellation, BlackBerry and Descartes dropped between 2.3% and 6%. Financial stocks, which include the big banks, fell 1.3%. In the US, the Nasdaq lost 626.51 points on Monday.
Why it matters: Nvidia, the giant US chipmaker at the centre of the AI boom, reports results after markets close today. It's the first report since CEO Jensen Huang resigned last week. The company has lost about US$410 billion in value since then. AI company Anthropic is also set to start trading on the Nasdaq on Nov 19. Michael Dehal of Raymond James said, "Right now the sentiment is pretty weak for the equity market."
What it means for you: If you own index funds or a retirement account, you've probably seen a dip this week. Moves like this are common around big earnings reports. It's usually not a reason to make sudden changes to a long-term plan.
US / Global

US rate hike now looks almost certain, and the loonie stays under 70 US cents

What happened: Traders now see a 95.4% chance that the US Federal Reserve raises rates by 0.25 points in December, according to CME FedWatch (via The Globe and Mail). Fed officials hinted last week they may be open to raising rates faster. The Canadian dollar closed Monday at 69.94 US cents, down from 70.14 cents on Friday.
Why it matters: When US rates rise, saving and investing in US dollars pays more. That tends to pull money away from the Canadian dollar. The Bank of Canada makes its own rate decision on Dec 9. It raised its rate to 2.5% on Oct 28, its first hike in three years.
What it means for you: A weaker loonie makes US trips, online shopping from US stores, and imported food more expensive. If you have a variable-rate mortgage, keep an eye on Dec 9. A Canadian hike would raise your payments.
Canada

Home building slowed in October

What happened: Canada Mortgage and Housing Corp. (CMHC) said today that the yearly pace of new homes being started fell 2% in October. It was 244,558 units, down from 250,072 in September. The six-month average fell 1.7% to 246,231. Ontario, British Columbia and Atlantic Canada drove the drop.
Why it matters: Building more homes is the main way to make housing more affordable over time. CMHC's deputy chief economist Kevin Hughes said the results "signal reduced momentum for housing construction." There was some good news: the actual number of homes started in larger communities was 21,059. That's 6% more than in October 2025.
What it means for you: If you're hoping to buy or rent, slower building means less new supply. That could keep prices and rents high in big cities, especially in Ontario and B.C.
VC / Private Markets

Startup funding is flat this year, but money for young companies is drying up

What happened: The Canadian Venture Capital and Private Equity Association (CVCA) says Canadian startups raised $4.3 billion over 397 deals in the first nine months of 2026. That's about level with last year, down 1% in dollars and 3% in number of deals. In the third quarter alone, startups raised $1.35 billion. That's 34% less than a year earlier. Most of that drop comes from one huge Cohere deal in 2025.
Why it matters: The money is going to fewer, bigger companies. Deals of $50 million or more made up 58% of all dollars invested. Funding for early-stage companies in the third quarter was just $380 million. That's the lowest quarterly total since 2021. Funding for growing mid-size companies fell 55% from last year. No Canadian startup has gone public on the stock market this year.
What it means for you: If you work at a startup or are thinking of joining one, raising money is hard right now, especially for mid-size companies. Ask about the company's runway: how many months it can run on the cash it has.

Learn One Thing Today
Seasonally Adjusted Annual Rate
A way to report a single month's number as if that pace lasted a full year, after removing normal seasonal ups and downs. For example, fewer homes get started in winter just because of the cold. "Seasonally adjusted" removes that effect, so you can fairly compare one month with another. "Annual rate" turns the month into a yearly figure. Here's why you should care: when CMHC says housing starts were 244,558 in October, that doesn't mean 244,558 homes were started that month. It means October's pace, if it kept up all year, would produce that many homes. That's how you can tell whether home building is really speeding up or slowing down.
