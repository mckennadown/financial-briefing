Developments
Canada

TSX falls for a sixth day in a row, its longest losing streak since 2024

What happened: The TSX fell 549.32 points on Thursday to 34,028.73, Reuters reported. That's its lowest close since May 19. Gold fell 1.6% to a 14-month low. Mining stocks fell 2.9% and tech stocks 3.3%. Seabridge Gold dropped 29.3% and Shopify 5.2%. One bright spot: Telus jumped 13.7% after a report that BCE has held early talks with the company.
Why it matters: Six straight losses is the TSX's longest losing run since December 2024. Gold has been a big driver of Canadian stocks, so its slide hurts. Investors expect the US Fed to raise rates in December. That boosts the US dollar, which usually pushes gold down.
What it means for you: If you hold a Canadian index fund, it's had a rough week. If you own Telus or BCE shares, watch for news on whether the talks become a real deal. Long-term investors don't need to act on a single bad week.
US / Global

US stocks drop more than 2% as worries about AI stocks spread

What happened: The S&P 500 fell about 2% to 7,175.83 on Thursday, its sixth straight loss. The Nasdaq fell 2.3%, and CNBC reported it's now down for 2026. The Dow closed below 50,000 for the first time since June. The selloff came on the day AI company Anthropic listed its shares, which CNBC called the biggest-ever IPO (a company's first sale of shares to the public). Its shares rose 17% on day one, but other stocks still fell. Reuters said US stock futures fell again Friday morning.
Why it matters: A handful of big AI and tech companies make up a large part of US stock indexes. When investors start doubting them, the whole market feels it. Many Canadians own US stocks through funds and pension plans.
What it means for you: If your portfolio leans on US tech funds, it's likely down this week. Make sure you're not overly concentrated in one theme. Checking your mix of investments at least once a year helps avoid surprises.
Canada

Retail sales rose 0.8% in September, but mostly because of gas prices

What happened: Statistics Canada said Friday that retail sales rose 0.8% in September to $75.3 billion, Reuters reported. Gas station sales jumped 6.9%. Without gas stations and car dealers, sales rose just 0.1%. After removing price increases, Canadians bought only 0.1% more stuff. An early estimate suggests sales fell 1.5% in October.
Why it matters: The headline number looks strong, but most of the gain came from higher prices, not people buying more. And October may be much weaker. The Bank of Canada watches spending closely when it sets interest rates.
What it means for you: Higher prices at the pump are eating into household budgets. A weaker October could show people pulling back. That could make the Bank of Canada more careful about raising rates.
VC / Private Markets

Toronto's Odaia raises US$15 million and signs a US partner

What happened: Odaia, a University of Toronto spinout, raised a US$15 million (about C$21 million) Series C round, BetaKit reported. Monograph Capital led it. US agency Publicis Health Media also invested and became Odaia's exclusive US agency partner. Earlier backers returned, including BDC Capital, the venture arm of the federal Business Development Bank of Canada. Odaia's AI helps drug companies decide which doctors to reach and what to tell them.
Why it matters: Later-stage funding has been hard to find in Canada. This round also comes with a US commercial deal, which gives a Canadian company a direct route into the world's biggest health market.
What it means for you: Canadian tech companies that win US customers while staying here create higher-paying jobs at home. BDC's stake also means some public money is backing homegrown AI.

Learn One Thing Today
Nominal vs. Real (Volume)
"Nominal" numbers are measured in dollars, so they go up when prices rise even if nothing else changes. "Real" numbers, sometimes called "volume," take price changes out to show how much stuff was actually bought. Today's retail report shows why the difference matters. Nominal sales rose 0.8% in September, but real sales rose only 0.1%. Most of the gain came from higher prices, especially for gas. Here's a simple example: if you spend $60 to fill your tank instead of $55, your spending went up, but you didn't get any more gas.
