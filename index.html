Developments
Canada

Inflation holds at 3.1%, but gas is doing most of the work

What happened: Statistics Canada said on Monday that prices rose 3.1% over the past year in October. That's the same as September, and slightly below the 3.2% economists expected. Gasoline cost 30.9% more than a year ago. Grocery prices rose 2%, a faster pace after four months of slowing. If you take energy out, inflation was just 2.0%, down from 2.1%.
Why it matters: The Bank of Canada aims to keep inflation at 2%. Outside of energy, prices are roughly on target. Desjardins says the average of the bank's two favourite "core" measures fell to 1.9% from 2.0%. Shelter costs rose only 1.4%, and rent rose 1.7%, both slower than before.
What it means for you: Filling up the car is still much pricier than last year. But most other costs, including rent, are rising more slowly. Grocery bills are creeping up again, so it's worth watching flyers and store brands.
Canada

A December rate hike is now a coin flip

What happened: After the inflation report, traders saw roughly even odds that the Bank of Canada raises rates on Dec 9, according to market data from Bloomberg (via The Globe and Mail). Canada's two-year government bond yield fell about 0.04 points to 3.317%. Economists are split. "There's nothing screaming rate hike in this report," said Benjamin Reitzes of BMO. Derek Holt of Scotiabank called it "probably good enough to hike in December."
Why it matters: The bank raised its rate to 2.5% on Oct 28, its first hike in three years. Another hike would make borrowing more expensive right before the holidays. Desjardins notes hiring has fallen for three straight months, which argues for waiting.
What it means for you: If you have a variable-rate mortgage or line of credit, your payments could rise after Dec 9, or stay put. Two-year bond yields help set fixed mortgage rates, so the small drop is mildly good news if you're renewing soon.
US / Global

Nvidia beats expectations, but the market's mood stays sour

What happened: Nvidia reported sales of US$115.84 billion for its latest quarter after markets closed Tuesday. That's over US$6 billion more than analysts expected, according to LSEG data (via CNBC). It also forecast US$130 billion next quarter, above the US$125.1 billion expected. Its shares rose nearly 4% before markets opened Wednesday, but were down more than 2% by late morning. Earlier, on Tuesday, the TSX fell 406.83 points to 35,315.30, its fourth losing day in a row.
Why it matters: Nvidia is the chipmaker at the centre of the AI boom. It was its first report since CEO Jensen Huang resigned last week. Strong numbers weren't enough to lift the mood. Other AI-related stocks like CoreWeave fell more than 6% on Wednesday. On Tuesday, the S&P 500 closed at its lowest level since July 29, CNBC reported.
What it means for you: Most Canadian retirement accounts hold US tech stocks through index funds. Swings like this are normal when investors are nervous. A good report not lifting a stock is a sign that worry, not the numbers, is driving prices for now.
VC / Private Markets

Ottawa's $1.75-billion startup funding plan: feedback closes Nov 23

What happened: The federal government's new Venture and Growth Capital Plan puts $1.75 billion into Canadian startup funds. The government expects it to draw at least $3.8 billion more from private investors. About $675 million targets later-stage companies (Series B and beyond), and $75 million goes to the very youngest ones. The deadline for public feedback on how the later-stage money should work is Nov 23.
Why it matters: Yesterday's briefing showed money for young and mid-size Canadian startups is drying up. This plan aims at exactly that gap. Its biggest piece, $700 million, must be matched by at least $3 of private money for every $1 from Ottawa. The Business Development Bank of Canada (BDC) will manage it. "We're betting that Canadian companies can start, can scale, and can stay here," said Industry Minister Mélanie Joly.
What it means for you: If you work at or are starting a company, more funding could reach Canadian investors in 2027. If you have views on how public money should back startups, you can still send them before Nov 23.

Learn One Thing Today
Core Inflation
A measure of price increases that strips out the items whose prices jump around the most, like gasoline. The Bank of Canada watches two main versions. "CPI-trim" drops the biggest price rises and falls each month. "CPI-median" picks the price change right in the middle. Here's why you should care: headline inflation was 3.1% in October, mostly because gas cost 30.9% more than a year ago. But core inflation averaged just 1.9%. That tells the Bank of Canada that rising prices aren't spreading widely through the economy. That gap is a big reason why economists can't agree on whether rates will go up in December.
